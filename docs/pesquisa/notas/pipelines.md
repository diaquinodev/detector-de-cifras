# Chord-recognition pipelines and reusable code

Scope: published pipelines from 2010–2012 (NNLS chroma, Chordino, HarmTrace / MPTREE) and the public Chordify descriptions through 2020, plus the repositories named in the assignment as they stand in package metadata fetched in November 2026. Nothing here is a recipe for copying Chordify’s or any other commercial network. Chordify’s current weights are not public, and this note does not look for them.

Dates below are taken from the fetched page or API payload. Where a page showed a day and month and omitted the year, the year is not filled in.

## What do Chordino / NNLS Chroma, HarmTrace, and the later Chordify descriptions actually do?

### Takeaway

NNLS chroma (2010) is a classical front end: tune a log-frequency spectrum, whiten it, solve a non-negative least squares note dictionary, then fold bass and treble into 12-bin chroma. Chordino, the shipped Vamp plugin, matches that chroma to a chord dictionary and smooths with a simple change rule or a small HMM. The 2010 paper’s 80% figure used a different, heavier dynamic Bayesian network, not that plugin. HarmTrace (2011–2013) is a symbolic Haskell grammar of tonal function, not an audio model. The 2012 MPTREE system is the audio pipeline that connects NNLS chroma, a bar/beat grid, binary templates, Krumhansl–Kessler key profiles, and that grammar. Chordify’s own 2019–2020 posts describe a later, unpublished pair of neural nets for chords and for beats/downbeats.

### Cited Findings

**NNLS chroma front end (Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010).** The method is older than 2012 and is the one the later plugin documents.

- Audio in the paper is downsampled to 11025 Hz. Each frame is a length-4096 DFT with a Hamming window and hop 2048 (about 0.05 s). Magnitude bins are mapped, by a single matrix that combines cosine interpolation on linear and log scales, onto centres spaced a third of a semitone. The tuned log-frequency spectrogram has 256 bins, about seven octaves. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §§2.2](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- Global tuning assumes equal temperament. The spectrogram, not the dictionary, is shifted by linear interpolation so that the centre bin of every semitone matches the estimated tuning. The tuning estimator itself is deferred to Mauch’s 2010 thesis. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §2.2](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- Pre-processing: a running mean over a Hamming-weighted, octave-wide neighbourhood (bins k−18 through k+18 at three bins per semitone). Method `sub` subtracts that mean and clamps negatives to zero. Method `std` also divides by the running standard deviation. Edge bins copy the nearest fully covered bin. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §2.3, equation 2](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- Note dictionary: 84 idealised tones, A0 (27.5 Hz) through G♯6 (about 3322 Hz). Harmonic k has amplitude s^(k−1). Tested shapes are s = 0.6, s = 0.9, and a linear spacing from 0.9 on the lowest note to 0.6 on the highest. Each column is normalised to sum to 1. The paper credits the geometric partial model to Gómez. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §2.4](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- Each frame solves non-negative least squares: minimise ||y − Ex|| with x ≥ 0. The paper uses MATLAB’s Lawson–Hanson solver and says that, because E is full rank and has more rows than columns, the solution is unique. The alternative “no NNLS” path copies the centre bin of each semitone. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §2.4](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- Chroma: the semitone activation is multiplied by a pitch-domain window (one mid-range window for treble chroma, one low window for bass chroma; the shapes are Figure 2, not a numeric table) and summed into 12 pitch classes. Beat sync uses Davies’s beat tracker, then the median across frames between consecutive beats, then division by the maximum norm. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §2.5](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- The high-level model in that paper is a dynamic Bayesian network with metric position, key, chord, and bass, observing treble chroma and bass chroma. The chord node has 121 states: 12 roots × 10 types (major, minor, major in first inversion, major in second inversion, major 6th, dominant 7th, major 7th, minor 7th, diminished, augmented) plus “no chord”. Decoding is Viterbi in Murphy’s Bayes Net Toolbox. This is an expert model, not a trained neural net. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, §2.1](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- On the 210-song MIREX 2009 collection, standardised NNLS chroma with s = 0.6 reached 80% MIREX overlap, against 79% for the same front end without NNLS and against the reported 74% top MIREX 2009 result. The finer 121-way metric peaked at 63.3% for linearly spaced s. Gains were largest on inversions and augmented chords (about 9–12 points); major triads barely moved. — [Mauch & Dixon, ISMIR 2010, Table 1 and §3.2](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)

**Chordino plugin (the code path a host actually runs).** This is simpler than the 2010 DBN, and the authors say so.

- The library is three Vamp plugins: Tuning, NNLS Chroma, and Chordino. Plugin version listed on the Isophonics page is 1.1. Authors: Matthias Mauch and Chris Cannam. Windows, macOS, and 32/64-bit Linux binaries are offered from that page. — [Isophonics, Chordino and NNLS Chroma](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html); [c4dm/nnls-chroma README](https://github.com/c4dm/nnls-chroma)
- Plugin front end: one channel; constant-Q-like log-frequency spectrum with three bins per semitone; tuning so centre bins (2, 5, 8, …) sit on semitones even when concert pitch is not 440 Hz; running standardisation (subtract running mean, divide by running standard deviation) as a whitening control. NNLS uses a dictionary of harmonic notes with geometrically decaying harmonic magnitudes. The semitone spectrum is multiplied by a chroma or bass-chroma profile and mapped to 12 bins, with optional L1, L2, or max normalisation. — [Isophonics plugin page](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html)
- Plugin parameters and the “generic pop song” preset: NNLS on, spectral roll-on 1.0%, tuning mode global, spectral whitening 1.0, spectral shape 0.7. A harpsichord preset uses whitening 0.4 and shape 0.9. Roll-on (0–5%, plugin default 0%) zeroes bins below the first bin whose cumulative energy exceeds that fraction of total energy. Whitening is the exponent on the neighbour standard deviation: 0 means no whitening. Spectral shape is s in s^(i−1), range 0.5–0.9, default 0.7. Local tuning is recommended only when pitch drifts (podcasts, a cappella). — [Isophonics plugin page](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html)
- Chordino does not run the 121-state DBN. It loads chord profiles from a file named `chord.dict`, computes frame-wise similarities, and smooths with either a “simple chord change method” or “a standard HMM/Viterbi approach”. The page calls both “simple (non-state-of-the-art!)”. A `boostn` parameter (0–1, default 0.1) raises the no-chord profile; the page warns that values above the default quickly label real chords as N. Outputs are a chord estimate, a harmonic-change value, and a MIDI-like note rendering of the chord including the bass note. — [Isophonics plugin page](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html); [c4dm/nnls-chroma README](https://github.com/c4dm/nnls-chroma)
- The Python wrapper’s documented example labels include `N`, `C`, and `Am7b5`, so a default Chordino dictionary emits more than major and minor. The dictionary file itself was not retrieved in this pass, so the full label set is not listed here. Wrapper defaults passed into the plugin are `useNNLS=1`, `rollon=1`, global tuning, `whitening=1`, `s=0.7`, `boostn=0.1`. — [chord-extractor Chordino source docs](https://ohollo.github.io/chord-extractor/_modules/chord_extractor/extractors/chordino.html)

**A second, incompatible analysis hop is used when HarmTrace’s audio system calls the same plugin.**

- MPTREE (2012) hosts NNLS Chroma in Sonic Annotator 0.6 at 44100 Hz, mono mix of stereo, Hann window of 16384 samples, hop 2048. That is not the 11025 Hz / 4096 / Hamming setup of the 2010 paper. — [de Haas, Magalhães & Wiering, ISMIR 2012, §3.1](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)

**HarmTrace grammar (symbolic; 2011–2013).**

- HarmTrace formalises Western tonal harmony as Haskell algebraic datatypes that behave as a context-free grammar. Input is a sequence of symbolic chord labels plus a key, in Harte syntax with durations. Output is a tree of harmonic function. It does not read audio. — [Hackage, HarmTrace](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/HarmTrace); [Computer Music Journal abstract, de Haas, Magalhães, Wiering & Veltkamp, 2013](https://direct.mit.edu/comj/article/37/4/37/94817/Automatic-Functional-Harmonic-Analysis)
- The simplified productions published via Koops’s thesis: a piece is one or more functional categories; a category is tonic or dominant; a dominant may be preceded by a subdominant. Scale-degree rules differ for major and minor. Chord class is one of major, minor, dominant seventh, or diminished, and that class constrains which rules apply. The package ships two grammars, Pop and Jazz. — [Koops thesis, Fig. 8 and §C.2](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12932/10526/H.V.%20Koops%20-%20A%20model%20based%20approach%20to%20automatic%20harmonization%20of%20a%20melody.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1); [Hackage usage example](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/HarmTrace)
- An error-correcting parser inserts or deletes chords so that every sequence receives some analysis. The Hackage example `C:maj D:min;1 G:7;2 C:maj;1` with the jazz grammar parses as a dominant preparation (IIm, V7) followed by a tonic. — [Hackage, HarmTrace](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/HarmTrace); [ISMIR 2012, §3.3](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- The model extends Rohrmeier’s generative syntax: tonic and dominant spans, an optional subdominant before a dominant, then scale-degree nodes, with room for secondary dominants, tritone substitutions, diatonic fifth chains, and diminished-seventh transpositions. — [ISMIR 2012, §3.3](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)

**MPTREE, the 2012 audio system that uses that grammar (older method).**

- Front end is the NNLS Chroma Vamp plugin (bass and treble) plus the Queen Mary Bar and Beat Tracker, which supplies beat times and the beat’s position inside the bar. Chroma is beat-synchronised by averaging, not by the median used in the 2010 paper. — [ISMIR 2012, §§3.1–3.2](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- Chord dictionary: binary 12-vectors. The study uses major, minor, and dominant seventh, rotated over 12 roots (48 chords), plus an all-zero no-chord vector. The bass is a separate one-hot 12-vector matched to the bass chroma. Diminished sevenths are omitted as uncommon in pop. Distance is Euclidean. Distances are divided by the best candidate’s distance so the best score is 1. Candidates are kept down to a cutoff of 0.9, chosen by experiment. — [ISMIR 2012, §3.2 and Table 1](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- Key: a single tuned chroma without the NNLS activation step, correlated (Pearson r) with the Krumhansl–Kessler major and minor probe-tone profiles, 24 keys per beat. A Temperley-style dynamic program penalises modulation. The published recursion uses penalty p = 1, and key segments are forced to last at least 16 beats. — [ISMIR 2012, §3.4](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- Grouping: adjacent candidate lists are merged by intersection when the intersection is non-empty, and the left list must sit on beat 1 or 3 of the bar. Sequences are then split at key changes and at beats where a I or V candidate appears, capped at 12 chords and 30 expansions. The grammar picks the sequence with the lowest insertion/deletion error ratio, then the smallest tree, then the shallowest tree. For this pop corpus the authors removed three jazz specifications and added two blues rules (dominant sevenths on IV and I may act as subdominant and tonic). — [ISMIR 2012, §§3.5–3.6](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- On 217 songs (179 Beatles, 20 Queen, 18 Zweieck), relative correct overlap was SIMPLE 0.688, GROUP 0.736, MPTREE 0.739, MPTREEkey (ground-truth key) 0.741. Friedman test χ²(3, N=217) = 339, p < 0.0001. GROUP vs MPTREE and MPTREE vs MPTREEkey were not significant; MPTREEkey vs GROUP was. The authors’ own reading is that most of the gain is the metrical merge, and the grammar helps once the key is right. Evaluation vocabulary is the 24 major/minor labels plus N: dominant-class chords are scored as major. — [ISMIR 2012, Table 3 and §§4–6](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- Haskell sources for that era were pointed at Hackage `HarmTrace-2.0`. — [ISMIR 2012, footnote 10](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)

**What Chordify has said in public about leaving that stack.**

- The NNLS project page still lists “Chordify performs chord recognition based on NNLS Chroma” among services. That page is a plugin catalogue, not a 2026 system description, and it conflicts with Chordify’s own later posts. — [code.soundsoftware.ac.uk, NNLS Chroma and Chordino](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/projects/nnls-chroma)
- A 2019 public comment by someone identifying themselves as running Chordify says the first computational machinery was HarmTrace, used for similarity and also for chord recognition, and that HarmTrace “has been replaced by advanced machine learning models that learn harmony patterns from countless expert harmony annotations.” This is a forum post, not a paper. — [r/musictheory, 2019](https://www.reddit.com/r/musictheory/comments/b076de/functional_harmony_analysis_applied_in_practice/)
- Chordify’s algorithm post: audio is turned into a spectrogram; two deep networks are trained separately, one to emit chords and one to emit beat positions, on “thousands and thousands of songs.” No layer sizes, no chord vocabulary, no dictionary, no grammar, and no weights are given. — [Chordify, “AI-technology behind the chords”](https://chordify.net/pages/technology-algorithm-explained/)
- The 2019 ISMIR presence, as described on Chordify’s blog, was a semi-automatic beat-annotation corrector: it “automatically correct[s] ninety-nine percent of the wrongly placed taps,” plus a visualisation for the remaining taps. The blog presents this as annotation tooling at ISMIR 2019 in Delft, and recalls a first beta at ISMIR 2012 in Porto. It does not specify a chord-network architecture. — [Chordify, ISMIR 2019 beat-tracking post](https://chordify.net/pages/ismir-2019-beat-tracking-algorithm/)
- The August 2020 post is more specific about the production split and still gives no weights: a chord network and a beat network; the beat tracker also marks the downbeat (the “1” of the bar). A four-day run on one server trained the downbeat tracker on 4238 songs by comparing outputs with human annotations and back-propagating. Cached songs are served from a database; uncached audio is analysed on demand. — [Chordify, “summer bootcamp”, 19 Aug 2020](https://chordify.net/pages/algorithm-summer-bootcamp/)

### Inferences

- A local NumPy/SciPy reimplementation that people will recognise as “Chordino-like” is the plugin path: tuned log-frequency spectrum, running standardisation, NNLS with geometric partials at s ≈ 0.7, bass and treble fold, dictionary dot-products or distances, then a tiny HMM. Reimplementing the 2010 DBN is a separate, larger job; the ISMIR paper specifies the random variables and the 121 labels, and leaves the numeric transition tables in the thesis.
- The 2012 numbers say the grammar is a small refinement on top of beat-synchronous template matching. For a small local project, the metrical constraint (prefer changes on strong beats, merge stable beats) is the part of HarmTrace’s audio system with the clearest measured effect. Porting the full Haskell grammar is optional and is not required to get that effect.
- Chordify’s public 2019–2020 description is a trained chord net plus a trained beat/downbeat net. There is no published dictionary or grammar to reimplement from those posts. The NNLS project page’s claim that Chordify uses NNLS chroma should be treated as historical.

### Gaps

- The numeric bass and treble pitch windows (Figure 2 of the 2010 paper) and the contents of `chord.dict` were not extracted. The tuning estimator’s exact peak-picking formula is in Mauch’s thesis, which was not fetched.
- The 2010 DBN transition probabilities and Gaussian parameters were not fetched.
- Krumhansl–Kessler’s twelve-number profiles are cited by the 2012 paper and were not copied out here.
- No Chordify ISMIR paper PDF with layer shapes or a training-set definition was found in this pass. The 2019 blog describes a beat-annotation tool, not the chord network. Private weights were intentionally not sought.
- Hackage still documents HarmTrace; this pass did not check the package’s last upload date or whether `haas/harmtrace` has commits after 2013. — [GitHub haas/harmtrace](https://github.com/haas/harmtrace)

## Which of the named repositories are maintained in 2025–2026, and what do they need?

### Takeaway

Of the nine named codebases, three had a release or a meaningful commit in 2025–2026: Sonic Annotator 1.7 (May 2025), chord-extractor 0.1.3 (August 2025), and omnizart 0.6.3 (May 2026). Essentia’s Python package was republished in May 2026, but only as macOS and Linux wheels. None of the chord paths is a pure NumPy/SciPy install on this Windows machine: they ask for a Vamp DLL, a C++/Cython extension, librosa (and therefore numba), or a trained TensorFlow/Keras/ONNX model. basic-pitch’s latest release is August 2024 and it transcribes notes, not chord labels.

## Which pieces can be reimplemented with NumPy and SciPy only?

### Takeaway

NNLS templates, a Gómez-style HPCP, beat-synchronous binary (or weighted) chord templates, and Krumhansl-style key profiles are specified as linear algebra and are implementable with NumPy, SciPy, and an FFT. SciPy’s `nnls` is the Lawson–Hanson solver the 2010 paper used. Everything that is a Chordino DLL, an Essentia extension, a madmom Cython module, or a crema / basic-pitch / autochord / omnizart / Chordify network is a native binary or a trained model. Those can be used as references; they are not the NumPy reimplementation.

### Cited Findings

**NumPy / SciPy, no learned weights.**

- `scipy.optimize.nnls(A, b)` solves `argmin_x ||Ax − b||_2^2` for `x >= 0` with the Lawson–Hanson active-set method. That is the same problem and the same classical algorithm Mauch & Dixon cite (they cite the 1974 Prentice-Hall edition; SciPy cites the SIAM reprint). Per-frame use is one call per spectrum column. — [SciPy 1.18 nnls](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.nnls.html); [Mauch & Dixon §2.4](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf)
- The NNLS dictionary is a closed-form matrix: 84 columns, partial amplitudes s^(k−1), columns sum to 1, s in {0.6, 0.7, 0.9}. Running mean and standard deviation over ±18 bins, tuning interpolation, and the fold from semitone activations to 12 bins are array operations. The paper’s best reported MIREX setting is standardisation plus s = 0.6; the plugin’s pop preset is whitening 1.0 and s = 0.7. — [ISMIR 2010 §§2.3–2.5](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2010/Mauch-Dixon-ISMIR-2010.pdf); [Isophonics parameters](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html)
- HPCP, as implemented in Essentia and documented against Gómez 2006 and Fujishima 1999: take spectral peak frequencies and magnitudes; optionally whiten them; map each peak into a k×12 pitch-class vector relative to a reference frequency (default 440 Hz, called A3 in the parameter text); weight the bin with none, cosine, or squared cosine inside a window of `windowSize` semitones (default 1). Parameter `harmonics` (default 0) adds votes for hypothesised fundamentals. In the current source, harmonic n sits at `12 log2(n+1)` semitones and its weight is `1 / max(1, (semitone/12)*0.5)`, summed when two harmonics land on the same pitch class. Size must be a positive multiple of 12. Optional band split at 500 Hz, frequency limits default 40–5000 Hz, normalisation `none` / `unitSum` / `unitMax`. — [HPCP reference](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_HPCP.html); [hpcp.cpp](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/master/src/algorithms/spectral/hpcp.cpp); [MTG Vamp HPCP page, defaults differ: 120 bins, 50–5000 Hz, 8 harmonics, whitening on](https://www.upf.edu/web/mtg/hpcp)
- Spectral whitening in front of HPCP is also specified without a network: estimate an envelope in the spirit of Röbel & Rodet’s true envelope, then restate each peak in dB relative to that envelope, and convert back to linear magnitude. — [SpectralWhitening reference](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_SpectralWhitening.html)
- The Essentia chord stage after HPCP is a sliding-window match to major and minor triads only. `ChordsDetection` takes the HPCP sequence, assumes frame size = 2 × hop, and emits one label per frame from a window of `windowSize` seconds (default 2) plus a strength. Output names use flats for Bb, Eb, Ab and sharps otherwise (`A`, `Bb`, `B`, `C`, `C#`, …). `ChordsDetectionBeats` does the same between beat times. The tutorial pipeline is MonoLoader → FrameCutter 4096/2048 → blackmanharris62 → Spectrum → SpectralPeaks → HPCP. — [ChordsDetection](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_ChordsDetection.html); [tonal tutorial](https://essentia.upf.edu/tutorial_tonal_chords.html)
- Beat-synchronous templates in MPTREE are binary 12-vectors, Euclidean distance, divide by the best distance, keep scores down to 0.9, average chroma between beats, and merge lists that intersect when the left beat is 1 or 3. No learned parameters. — [ISMIR 2012 §§3.2 and 3.5](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- Key profiles: Pearson correlation of a 12-bin chroma (the 2012 system uses chroma without NNLS) against 24 rotated Krumhansl–Kessler profiles, then a dynamic program with modulation penalty 1 and a minimum segment of 16 beats. The procedure is NumPy. The profile numbers live in Krumhansl & Kessler’s probe-tone data, as cited. — [ISMIR 2012 §3.4](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf)
- A small chord HMM of the kind Chordino describes (frame similarities, then Viterbi) is also NumPy once the dictionary and a handful of transition probabilities are chosen. The plugin does not publish those transition numbers on the page fetched here. — [Isophonics Chordino section](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html)

**Trained network, or a native binary that merely wraps one.**

- Chordino / nnls-chroma as distributed is a compiled Vamp plugin. The algorithm above does not need that binary. — [Isophonics](https://isophonics.net/nnls-chroma.html)
- Sonic Annotator is a native host only. — [repo](https://github.com/sonic-visualiser/sonic-annotator)
- chord-extractor and autochord call that native plugin through `vamp`. autochord then requires its downloaded TensorFlow Bi-LSTM-CRF. — [chord-extractor source](https://ohollo.github.io/chord-extractor/_modules/chord_extractor/extractors/chordino.html); [autochord README](https://github.com/cjbayron/autochord/blob/main/README.md)
- Essentia HPCP and ChordsDetection are classical, but the Python entry points are a native extension, and PyPI does not ship a Windows wheel. — [PyPI file list](https://pypi.org/pypi/essentia/json)
- madmom’s chord programs load a CNN and a CRF shipped with the package, and the install compiles Cython. — [madmom chords docs](https://madmom.readthedocs.io/en/v0.16.1/modules/features/chords.html); [omnizart issue #6](https://github.com/Music-and-Culture-Technology-Lab/omnizart/issues/6)
- crema’s `ChordModel` is a TensorFlow/Keras structured-prediction model with a 602-way vocabulary. — [crema docs](https://crema.readthedocs.io/en/stable/models.html); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/crema/)
- basic-pitch is a trained transcription network (ONNX, TFLite, CoreML, or TensorFlow). It does not output chord symbols. — [README](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch/blob/main/README.md)
- omnizart’s chord command, as described by its Windows issue, still goes through Vamp and a TensorFlow/Keras predict. — [issue #6](https://github.com/Music-and-Culture-Technology-Lab/omnizart/issues/6)
- Chordify’s 2020 public description is two trained networks. No weights, topology, or dictionary are published there. — [Chordify algorithm post](https://chordify.net/pages/technology-algorithm-explained/); [2020 downbeat post](https://chordify.net/pages/algorithm-summer-bootcamp/)
- HarmTrace’s parser is not a neural net and not a binary, but it is a large Haskell grammar, not a NumPy routine. The 2012 pop edits (which jazz rules were removed, which two blues rules were added) are described in prose and footnotes, not as a copy-paste table. — [ISMIR 2012 §3.6](https://ismir2012.ismir.net/event/papers/295_ISMIR_2012.pdf); [Hackage](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/HarmTrace)

**Practical fit to a project that already has NumPy, SciPy, and imageio-ffmpeg, and cannot import librosa.**

- Librosa is a declared dependency of chord-extractor, crema, basic-pitch, and autochord. Those four are unusable here for that reason alone, before Windows Vamp or TensorFlow issues. — [chord-extractor requires](https://pypi.org/pypi/chord-extractor/json); [crema requires](https://pypi.org/project/crema/); [basic-pitch pyproject](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch/blob/main/pyproject.toml); [autochord requires](https://pypi.org/pypi/autochord/json)
- imageio-ffmpeg can replace librosa’s loader for the classical path, because NNLS and HPCP start from a PCM waveform and an FFT. That substitution is an inference from the papers’ inputs (a waveform, a sample rate, a frame length), not a claim that any of these repositories already call imageio-ffmpeg.
- Essentia’s later Key work is not the 2012 Krumhansl procedure. Release notes say Key and KeyExtractor gained new pitch-class profiles, detuning correction, a low-energy HPCP gate, spectral whitening inside KeyExtractor, and a new default profile named `bgate`. Those constants were not copied in this pass. Reimplementing key for the local project should follow the 2012 paper’s Krumhansl–Kessler correlation unless those source constants are read on purpose. — [Essentia GitHub release notes](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/releases)

### Inferences

- The smallest faithful chord pipeline that stays inside NumPy and SciPy is: ffmpeg decode → log-frequency spectrum → global tuning shift → running standardisation → `scipy.optimize.nnls` against the 84-note geometric dictionary → bass and treble chroma → beat-wise median or mean → Euclidean or dot-product match to a hand-written major/minor/dominant-seventh dictionary → optional Viterbi with a hand-chosen self-transition. Key can be a parallel Pearson correlation against published probe-tone profiles, with a modulation penalty.
- HPCP is the alternative front end if the project would rather follow Essentia than Chordino: peak picking, whitening, squared-cosine pitch-class voting, optional harmonic folding, then a 2-second major/minor match. It does not, by itself, solve the inversion problem that NNLS was designed to reduce.
- Borrowing madmom, crema, basic-pitch, autochord, omnizart, or Chordify means shipping someone else’s trained model. That is outside the constraint, and for the first four it also imports librosa.

### Gaps

- Essentia’s “best matching” major/minor comparison is not given as a formula on the reference page (dot product, cosine, and Euclidean distance are all compatible with the words “best matching”). The C++ body of `ChordsDetection` was not opened in this pass. — [ChordsDetection](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_ChordsDetection.html)
- Chordino’s HMM transition matrix and the exact `chord.dict` rows were not opened.
- The numeric Krumhansl–Kessler vectors and Essentia’s `bgate` profile were not transcribed.
- Davies’s beat tracker and the Queen Mary bar/beat tracker are published algorithms with their own native plugins. This pass did not extract their steps, so a NumPy beat grid is a separate piece of work; the chord math above can take beat times from anywhere.
- Whether SciPy 1.18’s `nnls` matches MATLAB’s solver bit for bit on these spectra was not tested. Both document the Lawson–Hanson method. — [SciPy nnls](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.nnls.html)
